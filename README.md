Infectious material persisting in the environment can be carried between households along directed routes such as shared water. For patch models with a single transmission route, faster movement lowers the basic reproduction number $\Rzero$ and keeps it between the smallest and largest local values. We ask what survives when direct person-to-person coupling is added. In a $k$-house model with an exposed class, direct coupling and nonlinear environmental incidence, $\Rzero$ is the spectral radius of an explicit next-generation matrix. We prove five results. (i)~At every finite movement intensity, $\Rzero$ equals the reproduction number of a perfectly mixed system with a self-consistent effective removal distribution. (ii)~Over all directed networks, the closure of the attainable values of $\Rzero$ is an explicit interval whose endpoints are hub values; the best sink can differ from the house with the smallest local ratio. (iii)~If movement is symmetric and direct coupling is reciprocal, $\Rzero$ is nonincreasing and convex in the intensity; dropping either condition allows movement to raise $\Rzero$. (iv)~For two houses, $\Rzero$ is always monotone; when movement occurs in both directions, its direction is given by a closed-form sign criterion. (v)~For any number of houses, $\Rzero$ is monotone in the rate of each single directed edge; when the next-generation matrix of the network without that edge is irreducible, the edge is strictly helpful, strictly harmful, or neutral at all rates, according to the sign of a difference of reproductive values, and optima over box constraints lie at vertices. A nonmonotone response to the overall intensity therefore requires at least three houses and at least two edges; we give a three-house example and a five-house application.
